## Short answer

I would push back on Medium. Based on what's in the submission, the case for treating this as a higher-tier system is stronger than the submitter acknowledges. I'd flag it for re-tiering before pilot approval.

## Reasoning

The submitter's rationale rests on one argument: *the advisor is the recommender of record, so RolloverAssist is a "productivity tool" rather than a "recommendation system."* That framing has several problems worth surfacing.

**1. The use case is a regulated recommendation, not generic productivity.**
Rollover recommendations are a well-known high-scrutiny area in US wealth management (DOL fiduciary considerations, FINRA suitability, Reg BI). The output isn't a meeting summary or a code suggestion — it's the substantive content of a recommendation memo and a client-facing summary. The fact that a human signs it doesn't change the regulatory character of what's being drafted.

**2. The "advisor reviews and approves" defence is weaker than it looks.**
The submission itself notes that the draft "appears as the default content of the advisor's outbound document" and that reviewers preferred the AI draft over advisor-only drafts in 22/30 cases. That combination — default-populated content plus perceived quality — is exactly the setup where automation bias is strongest. HITL is a real control only if the reviewer is genuinely positioned and incentivised to dissent, and at ~40,000 conversations/year across 480 advisors that needs more than an assumption.

**3. Scale and client impact are non-trivial.**
~40,000 client-facing rollover conversations over twelve months, each touching retirement assets, is material both in volume and in per-client consequence. Most tiering frameworks weight scale and client harm potential heavily.

**4. Known failure mode is directly relevant.**
The FINOS AI Readiness Governance Framework explicitly calls out hallucination risks for LLMs in financial contexts (AIR-OP-004), including fabricated regulatory interpretations and false information in loan/advisory adjudication. RolloverAssist sits squarely in that risk pattern: an LLM generating fee comparisons, suitability rationale, and regulatory-adjacent language.

**5. Several controls described as "in place" are actually deferred.**
- AI Deployment Register entry — "will be completed on approval"
- Formal monitoring plan — "developed in the first three months post-launch"
- External red-team — deferred to first post-launch quarterly review
- Advisor training materials — in progress
- "Drafted by RolloverAssist" indicator — not yet implemented

For a Medium-tier system that might be acceptable. For a system touching retirement recommendations at this scale, deferring monitoring design and red-teaming until after launch is the wrong order.

## What I'd recommend you say back

Ask the submitter to either:

- **Re-tier to High** (or whatever your equivalent is) and meet the corresponding control bar before pilot, **or**
- **Justify Medium explicitly** against your firm's tiering criteria — not against the submitter's own framing — with particular attention to: regulated-recommendation character, automation-bias risk given default-populated drafts, scale, and the FINOS-style hallucination failure modes.

I'd also flag that the testing section (30 synthetic scenarios, no external red team, preference evaluation rather than accuracy/safety evaluation) is thin for either tier, and is something the second line should require regardless of where it lands.

One caveat: I don't have your firm's specific AI tiering policy in front of me, so I can't tell you which named tier this maps to. If you can share that policy or point me to it in the platform, I can be more precise.
