### Copilot

Here's my view on the tier classification.

## The submitter's argument

The Medium rating rests on a single claim: *the advisor is the recommender of record, therefore RolloverAssist is a productivity tool, not a recommendation system.*

That argument is doing a lot of work, and I think it's weaker than the form suggests.

## Reading against the tier criteria

Looking at the criteria from your policy (§4.1):

**a. Audience — Customer-Facing?**
The form frames RolloverAssist as "internal." But §2 explicitly says it produces *"a client-facing one-page summary"* that flows into *"the advisor's standard rollover workflow"* and appears as *"the default content of the advisor's outbound document"* (§6). The system's outputs substantially shape what the client sees. By the policy's own language — *"substantially shape what is seen by, clients of the Firm"* — this is **Customer-Facing**.

**b. Consequence of error.**
Rollover recommendations are a well-known area of regulatory sensitivity (suitability, fee comparisons, fiduciary considerations). A poorly reasoned rollover memo that gets lightly edited and sent can directly harm a client financially and expose the firm. This is materially more than a productivity nuisance — it sits closer to **Consequential Recommendations**.

**c. Reversibility.**
Once a client acts on a rollover recommendation, reversal is costly and sometimes impossible (tax events, surrender charges, lost plan features). **Low reversibility.**

## Where the "human-in-the-loop" argument strains

The submission treats advisor review as decisive. Two concerns:

1. **Default-acceptance dynamics.** §6 says the draft "appears as the default content" the advisor edits. The §7 evaluation found reviewers preferred the RolloverAssist draft in 22 of 30 cases. That is a system whose output advisors will be inclined to accept with light edits — not a blank-page tool. This is exactly the pattern your tier criteria are designed to catch.
2. **Scale.** ~40,000 rollover conversations / ~480 advisors over twelve months. Even a low per-case error rate compounds.

The advisor being the licensed representative of record is a **liability allocation** statement. It does not, on its own, reduce the system's risk tier under the criteria as written.

## My read

Under your policy as I'm reading it (Customer-Facing + Consequential Recommendations + Low Reversibility), this looks like a **High** tier system, not Medium.

At minimum, the Medium self-assessment needs to be challenged on the record before approval — the rationale in §4 does not engage with criteria (a), (b), or (c) directly; it argues a separate point about who signs.

## What I'd want to see before agreeing to even Medium

- An explicit answer to each of the three tier criteria (a/b/c), not just the recommender-of-record argument
- Evidence on the *default-acceptance* risk: in the 30-scenario eval, how much did advisors actually edit the preferred drafts?
- A view from Compliance / Legal on whether rollover memos drafted by an AI fall within existing suitability and fiduciary supervisory frameworks

Want me to look next at the controls in §5 and §7 against what a High-tier system would typically require, or focus somewhere else?

## 1. Pre-deployment validation — thin

**What's in the submission (§7):**
- 30-scenario internal evaluation
- Reviewers preferred RolloverAssist draft in 22/30 cases
- "No material factual errors identified"

**What High typically requires:**
- A **statistically defensible evaluation set** — 30 scenarios is a demo, not a validation. For a system touching ~40,000 conversations/year, you'd expect hundreds of cases stratified by client segment, plan type, account size, and known edge cases (in-service rollovers, Roth conversions, NUA, after-tax basis, etc.)
- **Adversarial / red-team testing**: prompt injection via uploaded plan documents, jailbreaks attempting to surface inappropriate recommendations, hallucinated fund names or fees
- **Error taxonomy and rates**: not just "no material errors" but categorised — factual errors, reasoning errors, omissions, tone/compliance issues — with rates and confidence intervals
- **Bias / fairness testing** across client demographics, account sizes, geographies
- **Independent model validation** (not the build team)

## 2. Grounding and factual integrity — unclear

**What's in the submission:**
- §2 mentions the model drafts memos; §3 lists data sources (CRM, plan docs, fund data)
- Nothing on retrieval architecture, citation, or verification

**What High typically requires:**
- Explicit **RAG architecture** with documented sources of truth for fees, fund characteristics, plan provisions
- **Citation / traceability** in outputs: every factual claim in the memo linked to its source document
- **Numerical accuracy controls**: fee comparisons and projections should not be generated by the LLM freely — they should be computed deterministically and inserted
- Defined behaviour when sources are missing or conflicting (refusal, flagging)

This is the single biggest gap I see. A GenAI system drafting fee comparisons without described grounding controls is the textbook rollover-recommendation risk.

## 3. Human oversight design — asserted, not engineered

**What's in the submission (§4, §6):**
- "Advisor is the recommender of record"
- Draft appears as default content, advisor edits and sends

**What High typically requires:**
- **Anti-automation-bias controls**: forcing advisors to confirm specific elements (the fee comparison, the suitability rationale) rather than approving the whole draft
- **Edit telemetry**: measuring how much advisors actually change the draft — if median edit distance is near zero, the human-in-the-loop claim is fictional
- **Mandatory disagreement path**: a frictionless way for the advisor to reject and document why
- **Training and attestation** for the 25 pilot advisors specifically on the system's known failure modes

The §7 finding that advisors *preferred* the AI draft in 22/30 cases should be treated as a warning signal, not a success metric.

## 4. Monitoring and post-deployment controls — absent

**What's in the submission:**
- 4-week pilot, no described monitoring beyond it

**What High typically requires:**
- **Production telemetry**: drift detection, output quality sampling, advisor edit rates, refusal rates
- **Periodic re-evaluation** on a refreshed test set (model behaviour shifts even without fine-tuning, especially with a vendor-managed GPT-4o)
- **Incident response process** specific to AI failures (a wrong fee comparison reaching a client is an incident — what's the playbook?)
- **Client complaint linkage**: a way to flag when complaints involve AI-drafted communications
- **Kill switch and rollback** procedures

## 5. Vendor / model risk — under-addressed

**What's in the submission:**
- Azure OpenAI, GPT-4o, no fine-tuning, tokenised client IDs

**What High typically requires:**
- Documented assessment of **model change risk** — Azure OpenAI model versions deprecate and update; what is the firm's process when GPT-4o is replaced?
- **Data residency and processing** confirmation for client data flowing to Azure OpenAI (even tokenised — the plan documents and CRM extracts likely aren't)
- **Contractual review**: training opt-out, data retention, breach notification, sub-processor list
- Behaviour on **service degradation** (latency, outages) — does the system fail safe?

The tokenisation claim also needs scrutiny. Tokenising client IDs doesn't help if the plan document and account context sent in the prompt contain identifying or sensitive data.

## 6. Regulatory and supervisory framing — missing

**What's in the submission:**
- No mention of suitability, fiduciary standard, Reg BI, or recordkeeping rules

**What High typically requires (financial services specifically):**
- A documented view from Compliance on whether AI-drafted rollover memos fall under existing **supervisory procedures** (correspondence review, suitability supervision, rollover-specific rules)
- **Recordkeeping**: are the AI drafts, the edits, and the final memos all retained per books-and-records requirements? For how long?
- **Disclosure**: does the client need to know the memo was AI-drafted? (Varies by jurisdiction and firm policy)
- **State-level considerations**: NY and Boston means NYDFS scope and Massachusetts fiduciary rule — both have views on rollover practices

## 7. Pilot design itself — weak as evidence

The pilot as proposed (25 advisors, 4 weeks, no defined exit criteria in what I've read) will not generate enough evidence to support a scale decision. Standard High-tier pilots specify:

- **Success and failure criteria** in advance (e.g., "edit-distance median > X", "advisor-reported quality ≥ Y", "zero category-A errors")
- **Sample size targets** for memos produced
- **Stop conditions** — what observations halt the pilot immediately
- **Decision gate** — who approves scale-up, on what evidence

## Summary of the largest gaps

If I had to rank them:

1. **Grounding architecture for fees and plan facts** — this is the regulatory blast radius
2. **Anti-automation-bias controls and edit telemetry** — the human-in-the-loop is the entire risk story; it needs to be real
3. **Validation rigour** — 30 cases is not validation
4. **Compliance/supervisory framing** — rollover memos are a regulated artefact, AI authorship doesn't change that
5. **Production monitoring plan** — currently absent

Based on the policy text I have in context (§8 Human-in-the-Loop):

## What the policy requires

Three distinct requirements:

**1. The arrangement must be described in the submission.**
Not asserted — *described*. "Advisor reviews each draft" is an assertion, not a description.

**2. A qualified human shall review each AI output before it is acted upon.**
Two elements here:
- The reviewer must be **qualified** (the submission needs to establish this — for rollover memos, that likely means the advisor is licensed and trained on the system's failure modes)
- Review must occur **before the output is acted upon** (i.e., before the memo is sent to the client)

**3. The HITL design must be documented in sufficient detail that the AI Governance Office can determine whether the reviewer is in a position to make an informed judgment.**
The policy specifically calls out two factors that must be documented:
- **Time available for review**
- **Information presented at the point of review**

## The explicit exclusion

The policy contains a critical second paragraph:

> *"An arrangement in which the AI output is the operational default and the human role consists of choosing not to override does not, in itself, constitute HITL for the purposes of this Policy."*

This is the part that directly hits the RolloverAssist submission. From §6 of the submission, the workflow appears to be:

- AI generates the draft memo
- Draft appears in the advisor's outbox as default content
- Advisor edits and sends

That is precisely the pattern the policy excludes. The AI output is the operational default; the advisor's role is to choose not to override (or to lightly edit). On the face of it, this fails the §8 test.

## What the submitter needs to provide

To satisfy §8 as written, they need to document:

1. **Reviewer qualifications** — who the 25 pilot advisors are, their licensing, and any AI-specific training
2. **Time available for review** — how long advisors actually spend per memo (not theoretical, measured)
3. **Information presented at review** — what the advisor sees alongside the draft (source citations? confidence indicators? the underlying plan data? flagged risk elements?)
4. **Why this is not "choosing not to override"** — what affirmative action the advisor must take that constitutes genuine review rather than passive acceptance

The §7 finding that advisors preferred the AI draft in 22 of 30 cases is, under the policy's framing, evidence *against* the HITL claim, not for it — it suggests advisors are inclined to accept the default.
