## Summary view

**I do not think Medium is well-supported on the face of this submission. The criteria in §4.1 point toward High.** The submitter's rationale relies on a framing ("productivity tool, not a recommendation system") that the policy criteria do not actually turn on.

## Walking through §4.1

| Factor | Assessment for RolloverAssist |
|---|---|
| **a. Audience** | The outputs "substantially shape what is seen by" clients. The client-facing one-pager is generated by the model and appears as the default content the advisor sends. §4.1(a) says Customer-Facing Systems are typically Medium or High. → points to **Medium/High**. |
| **b. Consequence of error** | A rollover recommendation is a textbook example of a consequential financial recommendation to a retail client (suitability, fees, tax treatment). §4.1(b) says systems producing Consequential Recommendations that are also Customer-Facing are typically **High**. |
| **c. Reversibility** | Rollover decisions, once executed, are difficult and sometimes impossible to unwind without harm (tax consequences, lost plan features, surrender costs). → low reversibility, pushes **up**. |
| **d. Population scope** | ~40,000 client conversations / ~480 advisors over twelve months. This is a large population, not a contained experiment. → pushes **up**. |
| **e. Regulatory sensitivity** | Rollover advice is one of the most regulatorily scrutinised activities in US wealth management. You'd want to check whether §7 of your policy expressly names this domain — if it does, that alone may force a High classification. |
| **f. FINOS risk profile** | I don't have the FINOS mapping in front of me, but generative drafting of client-facing investment communications plausibly engages several FINOS operational/regulatory risks (hallucination, regulatory compliance, information leakage). Worth a specific check. |

## Why the submitter's rationale doesn't hold up

The §4 rationale is essentially: *"the advisor is the recommender of record, therefore this is a productivity tool."* Two problems with that:

1. **§4.1(a) is about whether outputs "substantially shape what is seen by" clients** — not about who is legally the recommender. A draft that appears as the default outbound content substantially shapes what the client sees, regardless of advisor sign-off.
2. **The human-in-the-loop in §6 is weak as described.** The draft is the default; the advisor "edits as needed." There is no described minimum review standard, no attestation, no measurement of edit rates, no mechanism to detect rubber-stamping. Under a realistic operating model, the model's draft will often *be* the recommendation in substance.

## What I'd recommend you push back on

1. **Re-tier to High**, or ask the submitter to produce a substantive argument against each of §4.1(a), (b), (c), (d) rather than a single framing argument.
2. **Confirm §7 applicability.** I can see §7 is referenced as the regulatory-sensitivity carve-out but I don't have its contents — if rollover advice is listed there, tier is settled.
3. **If it stays Medium, the HITL arrangement in §6 needs to be materially stronger** — measured edit rates, explicit advisor attestation, and sampling-based QA — before that framing is defensible.

Want me to go deeper on any one factor, or move on to the controls in §5 / testing in §7?
