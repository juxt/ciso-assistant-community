### Copilot

Here's my view on the proposed Medium tier.

**I would push back on Medium. The submission's own facts point higher.**

The rationale for Medium rests on one argument: "the advisor is the recommender of record, so RolloverAssist is a productivity tool." That framing understates what the system actually does. A few observations:

**1. The subject matter is materially regulated and consequential.**
Rollover recommendations to retail clients are one of the most heavily scrutinised activities in US wealth management (DOL fiduciary considerations, SEC Reg BI, FINRA suitability). A drafting tool that shapes the rationale, the comparison of fees, and the client-facing summary is operating squarely inside a regulated recommendation process — not adjacent to it.

**2. "Drafts, doesn't decide" understates anchoring risk.**
The submission states the draft "appears as the default content of the advisor's outbound document." When 480 advisors are reviewing ~40,000 drafts a year under production pressure, the default text is the recommendation in practice for a meaningful share of cases. The §7 evaluation actually reinforces this: reviewers preferred RolloverAssist's output 22 of 30 times, which suggests advisors will rarely override it. That's influence on the recommendation, not mere productivity.

**3. Scale and client population.**
~40,000 client-affecting interactions per year and ~480 advisors is not a small pilot footprint at steady state. Even a low error rate translates into a non-trivial number of clients receiving sub-optimal rollover rationales.

**4. The controls described are not Medium-tier controls.**
A few items in §5–§8 read as Low-tier:
- "Basic usage monitoring" and "a formal monitoring plan will be developed in the first three months post-launch"
- No external red-team; deferred to a post-launch quarterly review
- Pre-launch review conducted by the building team (Wealth Engineering), with no independent second-line testing referenced
- The AI Deployment Register entry is not yet created

For a system touching fiduciary-adjacent client outputs, I'd expect the monitoring plan, independent testing, and register entry to be **pre-conditions for pilot**, not post-launch deliverables.

**5. Conflict-of-interest surface deserves explicit treatment.**
RolloverAssist compares an existing 401(k) against the firm's own IRA product. The submission doesn't address how the system is constrained from systematically favouring the in-house product in its drafted rationale. That's a specific, foreseeable harm that a Medium-tier assessment should have surfaced.

**My recommendation:** treat this as **High tier** for the pilot, with the option to step down to Medium after the post-pilot review if monitoring data, override rates, and an independent evaluation support it. At minimum, before approving the pilot I would require:

- An explicit statement of how the system handles the in-house-product conflict
- A pre-pilot independent (second-line or external) evaluation, not just Wealth Engineering's internal QA
- A monitoring plan covering override rates, drift, and content quality — defined before launch, not three months after
- Register entry completed before, not after, approval

### Copilot

Pulling directly from the policy text I have in context:

> "Where a Risk Tier requires a HITL arrangement, the arrangement shall be described in the submission. A qualified human shall review each AI output before that output is acted upon. The HITL design shall be documented in sufficient detail that the AI Governance Office can determine whether the human reviewer is in a position to make an informed judgment, including the time available for review and the information presented at the point of review.
>
> For the avoidance of doubt, an arrangement in which the AI output is the operational default and the human role consists of choosing not to override does not, in itself, constitute HITL for the purposes of this Policy."

Breaking that into testable elements:

**1. The arrangement shall be described in the submission.**
Not asserted — described. A one-line claim ("advisor reviews each draft") is not a description.

**2. A qualified human shall review each AI output before it is acted upon.**
Three components:
- *Qualified* — the reviewer has the competence to judge the output. For rollover content that implies the advisor understands fees, plan features, and suitability well enough to catch a fabricated figure.
- *Each output* — not sampled, not spot-checked.
- *Before it is acted upon* — review precedes the action (sending to the client), not follows it.

**3. The HITL design shall be documented in sufficient detail for the AI Governance Office to judge whether the reviewer can make an informed judgment.**
Two specific elements called out:
- *Time available for review* — how long does the advisor actually have, in practice, given their workload and the volume of drafts?
- *Information presented at the point of review* — what does the advisor see when reviewing? Just the draft? The draft alongside the source data it was generated from? Confidence indicators? Flagged claims that need verification?

**4. The anti-default clause.**
The policy explicitly excludes arrangements where "the AI output is the operational default and the human role consists of choosing not to override." This is the critical one for RolloverAssist.

---

**Applying this to the submission.**

The submission states the advisor reviews the draft before it is sent. That gets you to element 2 in form, but not in substance, and it fails several of the others:

| Policy requirement | Submission status |
|---|---|
| HITL arrangement *described* in submission | Not described — asserted in one line |
| Reviewer is *qualified* | Advisor training materials still in progress with Wealth Learning |
| *Time available* for review documented | Not stated |
| *Information presented* at point of review documented | Not stated — unclear whether the advisor sees source data, confidence signals, or just the draft |
| AI output is not the operational default | **The draft is the default outbound communication.** The advisor's role is to edit or approve before sending. |

The last row is the one I would put in front of the AI Governance Office first. The policy's avoidance-of-doubt clause is written specifically to exclude this design pattern. RolloverAssist generates a draft client communication; if the advisor's workflow is "review and send," and sending the draft as-is requires no affirmative action beyond clicking send, that is the operational-default-with-opt-out pattern the policy carves out.

To rescue it, the submission would need to show one of:

- The draft is *not* the default — e.g. the advisor must affirmatively compose or assemble the final communication from elements, not approve a finished artefact.
- Structural friction that makes review meaningful — e.g. specific claims (fee figures, plan features) are flagged and require the advisor to tick a verification box against the source before the send button activates.
- Or a re-tiering / re-scoping conversation, because the use case as designed may not be compatible with the HITL definition this policy uses.
